## Reviewer rules on identical games

I held our charges, logged median and eligibility gate, and opponents fixed. I compared two acceptance limits, 0.55 and 0.90 times each active line's median. With either choice, counterparties whose charges fall outside the interval produce exactly the same payment. For charges inside it, accepting saves half a fair charge or pays the capped amount of an inflated charge. The calculation retains uncertainty about hidden charges and caps. These are historical, conditional euro bounds. They describe incomplete observation, not sampling uncertainty.

| Rule | Games using 0.90× | Euros saved vs always using 0.55×, games 1–15 |
|---|---|---:|
| Always use 0.90× | All 15 | −9,532 to −7,932 |
| Use 0.90× when its cumulative lower-bound saving on earlier games with byte-identical policy wording is positive | 6, 11, 14 | −216 to −39 |
| Use 0.90× when its lower-bound saving on the immediately preceding game is positive | 3–7, 12–14 | +1,610 to +1,738 |

The policies use only earlier games to choose the factor, assuming their settlements are available before the next decision. The rules themselves were specified retrospectively. In games 10–15 alone, fixed 0.90× **saved €2,541–€3,832** against 0.55×. For the preceding-game rule, game 12 supplied €2,651. Removing that contribution with all choices held fixed turns the aggregate into **−€1,041 to −€913**. That is insufficient evidence for a rule that follows the last game.

The replay covers 62 printed positions across 15 games, with 31 positive logged limits and 8,184 unique directed settlements. It checks every expected team pairing against the roster, all team nets against the official matrix, and our income and costs against the archive. Logged medians are rounded. Attempted submissions supply candidate inputs only and never narrow the bounds. Replay assumes opponents would have kept their historical actions. With the repo environment and the complete external field cache, run `.venv/bin/python research/bench/policy_family_replay.py FIELD_DIR`. The paired calculation also contained the known difference in 60,000 synthetic comparisons involving hidden charges and payment caps. ## What Fable challenged

Fable identified a plausible error upstream of the factor. In its retrospective classification, our median exceeded the historical upper bound on 16 of 18 ancillary lines (examples include disposal and call-outs). On 11 of 19 principal lines it was below the lower bound. Fable proposed a zero reviewer limit on ancillary lines, 1.25× for principal lines in a previously seen scenario family, and 0.55× for other principal lines. Its labels were chosen after looking at outcomes, and its game 16 medians were inferred. Treat these counts as leads. [Independent advisor memo](06-fable-adaptive-review.md)

I translated that proposal into a rule we could reproduce without judgement after settlement. Its ancillary list is applied to the first logged reader's invoice descriptions. For the initial test, “seen family” means an earlier game with identical policy text. Against fixed 0.55×, it **saved €779–€1,932 on games 11–15** but **cost €2,837–€5,769 on games 1–15**. In game 9 alone, treating the surge electronics as familiar principal items cost €12,292–€13,289. An earlier case combined two devices on one line. That was enough for the policy to look familiar and too little to price either device separately. A different definition of scenario family would be a different experiment. [Replay and exact matching rule](bench/policy_family_replay.py) · [Game 1 invoice](../past-cases/case_001/invoice.txt) · [Game 9 invoice](../past-cases/case_009/invoice.txt)

Fable also suggested a strong diagnostic control. My complete-field replay independently confirmed that rejecting all positive incoming charges saved €5,116–€6,000 against 0.55× over games 1–15. Almost the entire advantage came from games 7–10 (€5,216–€6,100). The rule lost €158 in games 1–6. This makes it valuable as a shadow benchmark. Game 17's excess penalties illustrate the exposure if it were used live.

Two further qualifications matter. Games 19 and 20 had already settled when we received the memo, so any prospective block starts at the first game released **after** a genuine freeze. A later limit compatible with the observed charge cannot identify a median multiple without knowing the median. Fable’s approximate sample-size calculation also assumes game-level variability transfers across rounds. I did not use that calculation or inferred settings to select a live rule.

## A game-theoretic decision that fits the submission format

Let X be an opponent's charge, B its acceptance limit as reviewer, T the hidden fair threshold and C the hidden cap. Condition estimates on evidence available before submission. If T were known, setting the reviewer limit to T would minimize cost against every opponent by accepting every fair charge and rejecting every inflated one. With T uncertain, opponents determine which valuation mistakes cost the most. For expected net euros, each outgoing charge and incoming limit can be chosen separately because the round payoff is additive. One common limit must cover all opponents on a line. The rules give these two objectives. [Organizer payoff table](../materials/GAME_DESCRIPTION.md)

- **Reviewer.** Choose b to minimize E[1{X ≤ b} min(X,C) + 1{X > b} 1{X ≤ T} 1.5X]. Moving b only changes payments for X between the old and new limits. For a specific uncapped charge x, accepting breaks even at P(T ≥ x | X=x, case) = 2/3. Opponents read similar documents, so the amount they choose may itself carry information. A single cutoff must minimize the cumulative cost across feasible prices even when individual break-even decisions are not monotone.
- **Issuer.** Choose a to maximize E[1{B ≥ a} min(a,C) + 1{B < a} 1{T ≥ a} 1.5a]. An accurate fair charge can earn a premium from strict reviewers. Estimating T alone omits that incentive. Evaluate a small set of candidate charges against plausible opponent limits, including a shift toward stricter reviewing.
- **Strategic response.** Historical replay estimates a unilateral change with the old field held fixed. To study adaptation by opponents, evaluate the same small policy set against strict, lenient and changed mixtures. Populate each cell with a payoff range and identify policies that lose across plausible mixtures. A Nash equilibrium claim would require defensible payoffs for alternative strategies on both sides. Tournament rank also depends on which opponents gain or lose. [EGTA survey](https://strategicreasoning.org/empirical-game-theoretic-analysis-a-survey-2/) · [OpenSpiel payoff tables](https://openspiel.readthedocs.io/en/stable/alpha_rank.html)

## A working checklist from claims practice and evaluation research

1. **Read coverage and causation.** Identify the damaged object, cause, covered peril, selected endorsements, exclusions and already-damaged items. Quote the decisive case sentence and policy clause. Record ambiguity explicitly. Claims-adjustment guidance separates determining the loss scope from valuing it. The challenge's own policy governs each game. [California Department of Insurance property claims guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/res-prop-claim.cfm) · [QuantCo rules](../materials/GAME_DESCRIPTION.md)
2. **Build the payable scope.** Record the printed position, operation, attributable quantity and unit, repair or replacement basis, and any shared cap. Split labour from materials and one-off visits from per-unit costs. Apply VAT once. Check line and invoice totals. FEMA's published checklist similarly records item identity, quantity, age and replacement value. Its flood-specific policy terms belong to that program. [NFIP claims checklist](https://www.floodsmart.gov/claims-checklist)
3. **Check what the reader should do under a controlled change.** Switch an endorsement and check eligibility. Rephrase a line with the same scope and check that the amount stays stable. Lower a binding cap and check that the final payable amount cannot rise. These are functionality, invariance and directional tests from CheckList. The published open-source implementation is optional for tests this small. [CheckList paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.442/) · [CheckList code](https://github.com/marcotcr/checklist)
4. **Diagnose conditional errors in euros.** Predeclare a few line groups, including comparable price evidence, partial coverage, and bundled labour. Report games, families, positive lines, reviewer savings, issuer income bounds and the most influential game. Repeat with that game's contribution removed. Slice Finder formalizes the search for interpretable error groups. With this sample, keep discovered groups exploratory until fresh families confirm them. [Slice Finder study](https://research.google/pubs/slice-finder-automated-data-slicing-for-model-validation/) · [Authors' code](https://github.com/khtae8250/SliceFinder)
5. **Preserve the decision timestamp.** Record the active runner revision, resolved factors, reader outputs, per-line references, eligible actions, first on-time candidate and acknowledged payload. Replay in release order using only references already settled. Keep whole games together and distinguish another edition of a seen family from the first edition of a new one. The deterministic logs lack known randomization probabilities, which limits standard propensity-weighted off-policy estimates. [Rolling-origin evaluation](https://otext.robjhyndman.com/hyndsight/tscv/) · [Deficient-support analysis](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~tj/publications/sachdeva_etal_20a.pdf)
6. **Make opponents explicit when the simulation supports it.** OpenSpiel provides game representations and empirical payoff-table analysis. The immediate job here is to establish what the payoff entries can be. A small table in the existing replay is sufficient until competing strategy profiles can be simulated credibly. [OpenSpiel source](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel) · [Empirical game analysis](https://strategicreasoning.org/empirical-game-theoretic-analysis/)

## Can we see how much of the minute teams use?

**Opponent timing is unavailable from the public leaderboard.** I inspected the public page and the response fields behind its five views. Games expose scheduled start and status. Transactions expose issuer, reviewer, line, acceptance and payment. Performance, matrix and matchup views expose counts or euros. None reports team submission times, first attempts or overwrite counts. A first and only submission at second 5 produces the same settlements as an identical final submission at second 59. [Public leaderboard](https://c2f.public.quantco.cloud/leaderboard/) · [Organizer API handbook](../materials/API_HANDBOOK.md)

For our team, the tracked run timestamp minus the scheduled start was **12–35 seconds, median 21 seconds** in games 1–15. Games 1–6 were backfilled from our run logs. The statistic measures the local run timestamp. Across those games the median fastest completed model call took 9.64 seconds, which excludes case preparation and network submission. Neither number measures server receipt time. The organizer returns per-line `submitted_at` in each successful PUT response. The ensemble attempts an update after each member finishes, and currently discards that response. Keeping the first and last successful receipts would give our exact elapsed time and revision count. Anonymized first/final receipt offsets from the organizer would be needed to compare teams. The published payoff gives no bonus for waiting.
